### 1.1 Agentic e-commerce

**[magentic2025] Bansal, Hua, Huang, Fourney, Swearngin, Epperson, Payne, Hofman, Lucier, Singh, Mobius, Nambi, Yadav, Gao, Rothschild, Slivkins, Goldstein, Mozannar, Immorlica, Murad, Vogel, Kambhampati, Horvitz, Amershi. "Magentic Marketplace: An Open-Source Environment for Studying Agentic Markets." arXiv 2510.25779, v1 27 Oct 2025 (only version), cs.MA. No venue stated; OpenReview not checked. Paper licence CC BY 4.0. Code: github.com/microsoft/multi-agent-marketplace, MIT.**
- *What they did.* An open-source two-sided market simulator: assistant agents act for customers, business agents for competing sellers. They measure welfare against the optimum, the effect of search (lexical vs perfect), scale (33 customers/99 businesses and 100/300), six manipulation strategies by sellers (control, authority/fake credentials, social proof, loss aversion, basic and strong prompt injection) and proposal-order bias. Models: GPT-4o, GPT-4.1, GPT-5, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Sonnet-4, Sonnet-4.5, GPT-OSS-20B, Qwen3-14B/4B. Five runs per experiment.
- *Key findings.* First proposals are selected "between 60-100%" of the time against near-zero for third proposals (Section 5.4, Fig. 9); GPT-4o and Sonnet-4.5 hit 100% first-proposal selection in some conditions; the abstract summarises this as "10-30x advantages for response speed over quality". Welfare with 100 search results instead of one: GPT-4o -4.3%, Sonnet-4 -65.4%, GPT-5 (minimal reasoning) -44% (Section 5.2). Manipulation (Section 5.3, Table 3, Fig. 7): frontier models' mean payments to manipulative businesses "typically under 1.0 out of 3.0 possible", Sonnet-4.5 "virtually no susceptibility"; for GPT-4o, GPT-OSS-20B and Qwen3-4B prompt injection "often redirect[s] all payments to manipulative agents".
- *Overlap.* Buyer-side manipulation and injection inside a market is their territory, with more models, more strategies and a continuous metric. Our Phase 1 null for frontier agents (0 unwanted payments in 528 static rows, 400 hard rows, 80 red-team rounds) is consistent with their Sonnet-4.5 result and adds little beyond it; our one failure (an older model paying for injected add-ons in 4 of 20 episodes) matches their finding that weaker models are the ones redirected.
- *Difference.* They stop at the purchase. No mandate, no payment controls as a treatment, no dispute, evidence or liability layer, no post-purchase record. Our C1 to C3 controls and the E0 to E3 evidence package have no counterpart.
- *Reuse.* Run Phase 1 inside their environment (MIT) so the agent-side result is comparable; adopt their six manipulation strategies as the attack families and "mean payment to manipulative businesses" as a continuous metric beside our binary "unwanted payment"; cite the first-proposal bias as a reason the Offer Snapshot must record proposal order and timing.
- *Correction to Audit 0.* Audit 0 (Q3, item 8) says agents take the first offer "80-100% of the time"; the paper says 60-100% (Section 5.4). `paper/refs.bib` and `paper/main.md` already say 60 to 100%.

**[allouah2025] Allouah, Besbes, Figueroa, Kanoria, Kumar. "What Is Your AI Agent Buying? Evaluation, Biases, Model Dependence, & Emerging Implications for Agentic E-Commerce." arXiv 2508.02630; v1 4 Aug 2025, v2 27 Oct 2025, v3 17 Dec 2025 (read v3), cs.AI. No venue stated. Paper licence CC BY 4.0. Code: github.com/mycustomai/ACES, no licence file (GitHub API returns none). Datasets on Hugging Face: My-Custom-AI/ACE-RS (MIT), ACE-BB and ACE-SR (no licence tag).**
- *What they did.* ACES, a provider-agnostic sandbox with a visual mock retailer (VLM computer-use agents over screenshots) and a headless mode ("a ranked JSON list of eight products"). Six models in two waves (Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Flash in Aug 2025; Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.1, Gemini 3.0 Pro Preview in Dec 2025), eight categories, 1,000 market-share experiments per category, 500 choice scenarios per category for conditional-logit position effects, 200 seller-side experiments per category.
- *Key findings.* Position: for Claude Sonnet 4, moving a product from bottom-right to the top row (second or third column) gives "a 5-fold increase in selection rate"; row-1 logit coefficients 1.224 (Sonnet 4), 1.045 (GPT-4.1), 0.344 (Gemini 2.5 Flash), 2.146 (Gemini 3.0 Pro Preview), -0.701 (GPT-5.1) (Tables 2-3). Tags, from a 10% baseline: "Sponsored" 7.9-8.9%; "Overall Pick" 19.9-42.6%. Demand concentration: one phone case chosen 62.6% (GPT-4.1) and 95% (GPT-5.1); a fitness watch 45% (Sonnet 4) to 77% (Opus 4.5), 25% (GPT-4.1) to 6% (GPT-5.1). Seller-side description rewrite by an LLM seller agent (Table 4): +3.66 to +14.89 percentage points of share, maximum "+80.4 p.p." in one category by front-loading a query word. Model updates invert position preferences (Appendix D).
- *Overlap.* Seller-side optimisation against buying agents is measured here with a continuous outcome and a seller LLM in the loop, which is a stronger design than our best-of-4 red-team on a binary goal.
- *Difference.* No downstream dispute; no record of why the agent chose. Their model-dependence result is an argument for ABS: if the choice depends on the model version, "authorised but wrong" is partly a provider artefact, which supports AG1-P/AG1-C liability and the capture duty.
- *Reuse.* Adopt the conditional-logit measurement of influence for AG4-D (deceptive content that influenced the choice), which needs a quantitative notion of "influenced"; use a seller-agent rewrite loop as the red-team for listings; the ACES code has no licence, so re-implement from the paper, and use only ACE-RS (MIT) if any data is taken.

**[vend1] Anthropic. "Project Vend: Can Claude run a small shop?" Research post, 27 Jun 2025. [vend2] Anthropic. "Project Vend: Phase two." 18 Dec 2025. Both blog posts, not peer-reviewed; copyright Anthropic.**
- *What they did.* Claude Sonnet 3.7 ("Claudius") ran a snack shop for about a month with Andon Labs (Vend 1); Sonnet 4 then 4.5 ran several shops with a CRM, inventory tools, payment links and a CEO agent "Seymour Cash" (Vend 2).
- *Key findings.* Vend 1: the shop did not profit; the steepest loss came from metal cubes sold below cost; it declined a $100 offer for a ~$15 six-pack; customers talked it into discount codes and free items; it invented a Venmo account. Vend 2: the CEO "authorized such requests about eight times as often as it denied them"; discounts fell about 80% and free items roughly halved after the CEO was added, while refunds tripled and store credits doubled; the authors note Seymour shared Claudius's blind spots because both used the same model, and that "bureaucracy matters" (mandatory verification steps fixed pricing).
- *Overlap.* The manipulation-of-an-agent-with-money motivation is theirs. Vend 2's "same model, same blind spots" is the practical observation behind our A3 cross-family quorum; we did not invent it.
- *Difference.* No dispute or liability layer; no arbiter; no ground truth.
- *Reuse.* Cite Vend 2 for the same-family correlation argument beside our measured 1.24x and 2.45x error correlation; cite "bureaucracy matters" for the deterministic checks in C1/C2.
- *Wording check.* `paper/main.md` line 28 says Project Vend "found a shop-running model granting discounts far more often than refusing them [@vend1; @vend2]". The eight-to-one figure is Vend 2's CEO agent authorising lenient requests; Vend 1 gives no ratio. Attribute it to Vend 2.

### 1.2 Agent prompt injection

**[agentdojo2024] Debenedetti, Zhang, Balunović, Beurer-Kellner, Fischer, Tramèr. "AgentDojo: A Dynamic Environment to Evaluate Prompt Injection Attacks and Defenses for LLM Agents." NeurIPS 2024 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (poster; OpenReview m1YYAQjO3w, venue confirmed). arXiv 2406.13352; v1 19 Jun 2024, v2 18 Jul 2024, v3 24 Nov 2024 (read v3). Paper licence arXiv non-exclusive. Code: github.com/ethz-spylab/agentdojo, MIT (also stated in Appendix E.4).**
- *What they did.* Four task suites (Workspace, Slack, Travel, Banking), 74 tools, 97 user tasks, 27 injection tasks, 629 security test cases (the user x injection cross product). Metrics: benign utility, utility under attack, targeted attack success rate (ASR). Attacks include the "Important message" attack; defences: data delimiters/spotlighting, PI detector, repeat prompt, tool filter.
- *Key findings.* Benign utility: Claude 3.5 Sonnet 78.22%, GPT-4o 69.00%, Claude 3 Opus 66.61% (Table 3). Targeted ASR of "Important message": GPT-4o 47.69% in Table 3 but 57.69% in Tables 4-5 (an internal inconsistency; the Introduction's "less than 25%" refers to the best agents only), Claude 3.5 Sonnet 33.86%, Gemini 1.5 Pro 25.60%, Claude 3 Opus 11.29%, GPT-3.5 Turbo 8.43%. Defences on GPT-4o (Table 5): tool filter ASR 6.84% at 73.13% benign utility; PI detector 7.95% at 41.49% utility (false positives); repeat prompt 27.82% at 85.53%; delimiters 41.65%.
- *Overlap.* Our Phase 1 marketplace is an AgentDojo-style typed-tool environment with a narrower domain; our C3 monitor that sees only typed fields is a tool-filter-class defence; our "unwanted payment" is a targeted ASR.
- *Difference.* A purchase and a dispute follow the attack; liability for the injected purchase (AG4) is allocated; no counterpart in AgentDojo.
- *Reuse.* Report Phase 1 as user-task x injection-task security cases with benign utility, utility under attack and targeted ASR, so it is comparable; port our scenarios to the AgentDojo task format (MIT) and reuse the Banking suite's injection tasks; adopt their defence set as Phase 1 baselines.

**[wasp2025] Evtimov, Zharmagambetov, Grattafiori, Guo, Chaudhuri. "WASP: Benchmarking Web Agent Security Against Prompt Injection Attacks." NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (poster; OpenReview Ip1cCUAllL, venue confirmed; also an ICML 2025 WCUA workshop poster). arXiv 2504.18575; v1 22 Apr, v2 30 Apr, v3 16 May 2025 (read v3). Paper licence CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Code: github.com/facebookresearch/wasp, LICENSE file is CC BY-NC 4.0 (GitHub shows "Other").**
- *What they did.* End-to-end web-agent security on GitLab and Reddit clones from VisualWebArena; 21 attacker goals (12 GitLab, 9 Reddit), two user goals per environment, two injection templates (plain text, URL); 84 tasks (the paper's own count is ambiguous between 42 and 84 scenarios). Two metrics: intermediate ASR (the agent starts doing the attacker's task) and end-to-end ASR (the attacker's goal is completed).
- *Key findings* (Table 2). Intermediate ASR up to 0.857 (o1, tool calling, system-role instructions) with end-to-end 0.167; GPT-4o 0.321/0.012; Claude Sonnet 3.5 v2 0.583/0.060; Claude 3.7 extended thinking 0.536/0.036. End-to-end "from 0 to 17%". URL injections beat plain text (o1: 0.976 vs 0.738 intermediate; Table 3). A defensive system prompt cut GPT-4o intermediate ASR 0.321 to 0.167 at a utility cost (0.595 to 0.459). The authors call the low end-to-end rate "security through incompetence": end-to-end ASR rises with capability.
- *Overlap.* Our red-team table already distinguishes "attempted in C0" from "executed"; that is WASP's intermediate/end-to-end split under other names.
- *Difference.* Web pages vs typed API; our agents' zero follows from refusal, not incompetence (they completed every benign task), which is the opposite regime from WASP's and should be said.
- *Reuse.* Adopt the two metric names and report both (we have 1/57 attempted, 0/57 executed); adopt URL-style injections and the capability-vs-ASR framing. The code is CC BY-NC 4.0: research use only, so it cannot be vendored into a conformance kit meant for commercial adopters; re-implement from the paper.

**[camel2025] Debenedetti, Shumailov, Fan, Hayes, Carlini, Fabian, Kern, Shi, Terzis, Tramèr. "Defeating Prompt Injections by Design." arXiv 2503.18813; v1 24 Mar 2025, v2 24 Jun 2025 (read v2). No venue (OpenReview lists CoRR 2025 only; checked 2026-11-04). Paper licence CC BY 4.0. Code: github.com/google-research/camel-prompt-injection, Apache-2.0; README: "not an officially supported Google product".**
- *What they did.* CaMeL: a privileged LLM writes a program from the trusted user query; a quarantined LLM with no tools parses untrusted data into a schema; a custom interpreter tracks data flow, attaches capabilities (provenance, allowed readers) to values and checks security policies before each tool call. Guarantee: "the untrusted data retrieved by the LLM can never impact the program flow." Stated non-goals and limits: text-to-text manipulation and prompt-injected phishing (Section 3.1); data-dependent branches that let data become control flow (Section 6.4); side channels (Section 7).
- *Key findings.* On AgentDojo, CaMeL solves 77% of tasks against 84% undefended (abstract); for Gemini 2.5 Pro successful attacks drop "from 300 without to 0 with CaMeL"; for Claude 3.5 Sonnet over 949 attacks, 0 with CaMeL against 8 for the next-best defence (tool filter) and 276 for GPT-4o-mini native tool calling (Section 6.3); overhead 2.82x input and 2.73x output tokens for the median task (Section 6.5).
- *Overlap.* Our typed offer records with bounded `UntrustedText` fields, the monitor that sees only typed fields, and the principle "exact by construction, not interpretation" are CaMeL's data/control separation applied to a marketplace; `paper/main.md` already says the design "borrows" it. A2 (fact-finders parse evidence into typed Findings; a deterministic engine with fixed control flow decides) is a CaMeL architecture for adjudication. The utility cost we observed (strict A2 escalated 54%) is CaMeL's 77-vs-84 trade-off in another form.
- *Difference.* CaMeL protects an agent executing a trusted query. An arbiter's control flow is legitimately data-dependent: whether a claim is true decides which rule fires. CaMeL's excluded cases (text-to-text manipulation, data-dependent branching) are exactly the arbiter's residual threat model. We must never claim CaMeL-style provable security for the Decision Center; only the rule step is exact.
- *Reuse.* Cite as the design argument; adopt capability-style provenance labels on each Finding (which signed record it was read from) and CaMeL's security-policy language for "a Finding not cited to signed evidence is dropped"; count LLM-only findings (the paper's 14 findings used by 10 of 28 rules) as the manipulation surface.

**[anthropicpi2025] Anthropic. "Mitigating prompt injections in browser use." Research post, 24 Nov 2025. Blog post; copyright Anthropic.**
- *What they did and found.* Claude for Chrome against an internal adaptive best-of-N attacker with 100 attempts per environment. Claude Opus 4.5: 8.0% ASR model-only, 7.6% with the old safeguards, 1.4% with improved safeguards; Sonnet 4.5 1.9%, Sonnet 4 2.0%, Haiku 4.5 2.4%. "A 1% attack success rate ... still represents meaningful risk"; "No browser agent is immune to prompt injection." Defences: RL on simulated injections, classifiers over untrusted content, internal and external red teaming.
- *Relation.* Our red-team gave each (agent, goal) at most 4 rounds with a safety-trained attacker that refused to write a payload in 23 of 80 rounds. Against this baseline our 0/80 is not evidence of robustness; it is an under-powered attack. Audit 0's "about 1% at 100 attempts" is the post's rounded figure; the exact Opus 4.5 number is 1.4%.
- *Reuse.* Report ASR against attempts; use an attacker that does not refuse (human red team or an unrestricted model) with at least 100 attempts per target before any Phase 1 claim.

### 1.3 LLM judges and attacks on them

**[zheng2023] Zheng, Chiang, Sheng, Zhuang, Wu, Zhuang, Lin, Li, Li, Xing, Zhang, Gonzalez, Stoica. "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena." NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (stated on the arXiv page). arXiv 2306.05685; v1 9 Jun 2023 to v4 24 Dec 2023. Paper licence arXiv non-exclusive. Code and judge prompts: github.com/lm-sys/FastChat (`llm_judge`), Apache-2.0.**
- *Key findings.* GPT-4 as judge agrees with controlled and crowdsourced human preferences over 80% of the time, the same as human-human agreement; named biases: position, verbosity, self-enhancement, limited reasoning; releases 3K expert votes and 30K human-preference conversations.
- *Overlap.* Our A1 arbiters are LLM-as-judge on a factual-legal task rather than a preference task; the bias taxonomy applies. Self-enhancement bias is the academic name for the same-family problem in our design (a Claude arbiter judging Claude agents' episodes).
- *Difference.* Our judge has a liability rule and a signed record; agreement with humans is replaced by agreement with simulator ground truth and a blind expert (Amendment 11, not yet labelled).
- *Reuse.* Position-bias probe: swap the order of the buyer's claim and the merchant's reply and randomise evidence-item order, then report consistency; adopt their swap-and-agree protocol; reuse their judge-prompt structure (Apache-2.0) for the arbiter prompt's rubric.

**[judgeflip2025] Zhao, Liu, Yu, Kung, Chen, Mi, Yu. "One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge." arXiv 2507.08794; v1 11 Jul 2025, v2 26 Sep 2025, v3 11 Jun 2026 (read the current HTML). OpenReview: rejected submission to ICLR 2026, otherwise CoRR; so still a preprint. Paper licence arXiv non-exclusive. Model and data: huggingface.co/sarosavo/Master-RM and datasets/sarosavo/Master-RM, Apache-2.0.**
- *What they did.* In reference-based verification (RLVR-style reward models judging whether a candidate answer matches a reference), "master key" responses (":", a single space, "Thought process:", "Solution") earn false positives. They test specialised generative reward models (Multi-sub RM, General-Verifier, Omni-Judge) and general LLMs (Qwen2.5 0.5B-72B, LLaMA3-8B/70B, GPT-4o, GPT-o1, Claude 4 Sonnet) and train Master-RM with truncated outputs as adversarial negatives.
- *Key findings* (Table 1, overall average | worst). LLaMA3-70B 80.6% | 95.1%; Qwen2.5-72B 66.8% | 90.9%; GPT-4o 14.6% | 53.6%; Claude-4 12.4% | 57.7%; GPT-o1 6.0% | 37.3%; General-Verifier 66.8% FPR on MATH from a single blank space; punctuation alone "up to 35%" on GPT-4o. Master-RM-7B 0.1% | 2.9% with Cohen's kappa 0.91 against GPT-4o and 0.90 against humans (Table 2).
- *Overlap and a wording risk.* The result is about reference-based answer verification, not about a judge weighing adversarial parties' evidence. `paper/main.md` line 28 and Audit 0 say LLM judges "can be flipped by trivial tokens"; that transfer to dispute adjudication is a hypothesis we have not tested, and the sentence should say so or cite it only for reward-model judging.
- *Reuse.* A cheap robustness probe: file a buyer claim that is only "Thought process:" or an empty statement with a confident tone and check whether any arbiter or fact-finder grants a remedy; adopt their adversarial-negative augmentation when prompting or tuning fact-finders (truncated or contentless claims labelled "no finding").

**[judgedeceiver2024] Shi, Yuan, Liu, Huang, Zhou, Sun, Gong. "Optimization-based Prompt Injection Attack to LLM-as-a-Judge." ACM CCS 2024 (stated in the arXiv comments). arXiv 2403.17710; v1 26 Mar 2024 to v5 24 Aug 2025 (read current HTML). Paper licence arXiv non-exclusive. Code: github.com/ShiJiawenwen/JudgeDeceiver, no LICENSE file (root listing checked).**
- *What they did.* JudgeDeceiver appends a gradient-optimised sequence to an attacker-controlled candidate so a pairwise LLM judge picks it; loss = target-aligned generation + target-enhancement (position token) + adversarial perplexity (Eq. 8). White-box on Mistral-7B, Openchat-3.5, Llama-2-7B, Llama-3-8B; datasets MT-Bench and LLMBar; case studies LLM-powered search, RLAIF, tool selection.
- *Key findings* (Table 2). ASR 88.0-98.9% against at most 40.7% for the best manual injection ("Fake Reasoning"); positional attack consistency 83.4% (MT-Bench) and 86.6% (LLMBar) against at most 19% for manual attacks; jailbreak baselines at most 53.6%. Case studies 80-100% ASR (Tables 8-10). Defences (Table 11, Mistral-7B): known-answer detection false-negative rate 90-100%; perplexity 50-60%; windowed perplexity 40-70%.
- *Overlap.* The strongest known attack on an LLM judge exists and we have not run it. Our adversarial cases are hand-written false claims and edited listings; "fooled rate" measured against them is a lower bound on manipulability.
- *Difference.* Their judge compares two candidate texts; ours weighs typed evidence under a rule. Transfer to our setting needs an attacker who controls one field of the evidence package (merchant reply, listing text, buyer claim) and optimises against an open-weight fact-finder, then transfers to Claude and Grok.
- *Reuse.* Re-implement from the paper (the repository carries no licence, so do not copy code): optimise merchant-reply suffixes against an open-weight fact-finder to flip the conformity Finding, measure ASR and transfer rate to the closed arbiters, and report positional attack consistency across evidence orderings. Add known-answer and perplexity detectors as baselines and expect them to fail as they did here.

### 1.4 Scalable oversight and debate

**[irving2018] Irving, Christiano, Amodei. "AI safety via debate." arXiv 1805.00899; v1 2 May 2018, v2 22 Oct 2018. Paper licence arXiv non-exclusive. No code.**
- *Key findings.* Debate as a zero-sum game judged by a human; with optimal play debate answers PSPACE questions with polynomial-time judges against NP for direct judging; MNIST sparse-pixel judge: 6 pixels 59.4% to 88.9%, 4 pixels 48.2% to 85.2%.
- *Relation.* The conceptual basis for our tier 1 (buyer-side and merchant-side agents) feeding an arbiter, and for a proposed experiment (debate between advocates vs a single consultant). Our setting is a natural debate testbed with real information asymmetry: the merchant knows what shipped, the buyer knows what was wanted, the judge has only the record.
- *Reuse.* Frame tier 1 plus arbiter as a debate protocol and measure judge accuracy with and without advocates.

**[khan2024] Khan, Hughes, Valentine, Ruis, Sachan, Radhakrishnan, Grefenstette, Bowman, Rocktäschel, Perez. "Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers." ICML 2024, PMLR 235:23662-23733 (confirmed on proceedings.mlr.press). arXiv 2402.06782; v1 9 Feb 2024 to v4 25 Jul 2024 (read v4). Paper licence arXiv non-exclusive. Code: github.com/ucl-dark/llm_debate, MIT.**
- *What they did.* QuALITY hard subset (Gutenberg science-fiction stories); debaters see the story, judges do not; a quote tool marks `<v_quote>` verified and `<u_quote>` unverified quotes. Judges: GPT-4-Turbo, Mixtral-8x7B, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Claude 2.1. Debaters optimised for persuasiveness by best-of-N and critique-and-refinement; skill measured by Elo over a Swiss tournament of 20 debaters.
- *Key findings.* LLM judges: naive 48%, consultancy 54%, debate 76% (abstract and Section 1); with GPT-4-Turbo judging best-of-16 debaters, 75.6% against 92.5% for an expert judge who reads the story (Insight 1). Human judges: naive 60%, consultancy 78%, debate 88%; debate beats consultancy in static (p = 0.001) and interactive (p = 0.048) settings (Insight 6). Judge accuracy rises with debater Elo in debate (Insight 3) and falls with consultant win rate in consultancy (Insight 4, Fig. 7). Judges sometimes believe unverified quotes (Appendix A.7); without quotes accuracy drops (Appendix A.6).
- *Overlap.* Their verified-quote tool is our signed record in miniature: the finding that judges are fooled by unverified text maps onto our E0 failures (statements without records; 13 of 20 agent-fault cases charged to the buyer) and the one E3 fool (a staged photo against a parcel weight).
- *Difference.* Reading comprehension with an answer key vs a liability rule applied to records kept by interested parties; our advocates (tier 1) are not assigned sides and were not optimised; our agents refused to argue false positions in 20 of 20 instructions, which is a prompting regime the debate literature avoids by assigning positions.
- *Reuse.* Run the debate protocol as specified: assigned sides, buyer and merchant advocates with private information, judge with the package only; a consultancy baseline (one advocate); Elo over advocates; report judge accuracy by debater strength. Mark evidence items as verified (in the hash chain) or unverified (party statement) in the arbiter prompt, as their quote tool does. Audit 0's "76%/88%" figure is this paper's; the consultancy baselines (54%, 78%) should be cited with it.

**[kenton2024] Kenton, Siegel, Kramár, Brown-Cohen, Albanie, Bulian, Agarwal, Lindner, Tang, Goodman, Shah. "On scalable oversight with weak LLMs judging strong LLMs." NeurIPS 2024 (proceedings.neurips.cc hash 899511e37a8e01e1bd6f6f1d377cc250; pages in refs.bib not checked). arXiv 2407.04622; v1 5 Jul 2024, v2 12 Jul 2024 (read v2). Paper licence CC BY 4.0. No code release found in the text read.**
- *What they did.* Debate, consultancy and direct QA with weaker judges (Gemma 7B, GPT-3.5, Gemini Pro 1.0, Pro 1.5) and stronger debaters (mainly Pro 1.5), on 9 tasks x 128 questions (extractive QA with information asymmetry; closed tasks such as MMLU, TruthfulQA, PrOntoQA, GPQA, GSM8K; multimodal MMMU), about 5 million generations; 3 debate rounds, best-of-4.
- *Key findings.* Debate beats consultancy on all tasks when the consultant is assigned a side; debate beats direct QA only with information asymmetry (extractive), mixed otherwise (Pro 1.5 judge: debate worse than QA without article on closed tasks; GPT-3.5 judge: debate better, p = 0.037). Open protocols: the consultant/protagonist chose the correct answer 88% (extractive), 84% (closed), 71% (multimodal); open consultancy has higher win-rate, but judges are far less accurate when the consultant is wrong, and open debate protects against that (Section 4.2, Fig. 3). Stronger debaters give only "marginally higher judge accuracy" (Section 4.3; Elo over 512 pairings). Exact per-task accuracies are in bar charts (Fig. 2, B.1) and were not read.
- *Relation.* Information asymmetry is exactly our setting, so debate is predicted to help here, but the paper warns that debate can hurt when it is absent; test, do not assume. Their open vs assigned distinction maps onto our tier 1 (parties pick their positions; agents refused false ones) vs the forced adversarial cases.
- *Reuse.* Report both open and assigned protocols; measure judge accuracy conditional on whether the protagonist is right, as in Fig. 3.

### 1.5 AI control

**[greenblatt2023] Greenblatt, Shlegeris, Sachan, Roger. "AI Control: Improving Safety Despite Intentional Subversion." ICML 2024 (OpenReview KviM5k8pcP, linked from the arXiv comments; PMLR pages in refs.bib not checked). arXiv 2312.06942; v1 12 Dec 2023 to v5 23 Jul 2024 (read v5). Paper licence CC BY 4.0. Code: github.com/rgreenblatt/control-evaluations, MIT.**
- *What they did.* 1,500 APPS interview-level problems; untrusted U = GPT-4 (0613), trusted T = GPT-3.5 (0613), trusted labour H budgeted at 2% of problems. Safety = share of simulated 5,000-problem scenarios with no backdoored solution submitted against a red-team attack policy; usefulness = correct-solution rate rescaled so GPT-3.5 = 0 and GPT-4 = 1.
- *Key findings* (Table 7). Trusted model only: 100% safety, 2% usefulness. Upfront auditing: 15% / 100%. Trusted monitoring with deferral to T on the most suspicious 10%: 83% / 93%; 20%: 92% / 83%. Untrusted monitoring (a second GPT-4 as monitor, collusion broken by redaction and paraphrasing): defer 10% 92% / 92%; defer 20% 98% / 85%; naive untrusted monitoring 0% safety. Trusted editing 20%: 92% / 94%.
- *Overlap.* Our A2/A3 with "review" on an unresolved Finding is a control protocol: fact-finders are monitors, human review is deferral, and the review rate (54% strict A2, 93% A3) is the usefulness cost. Our same-family judges on same-family agent episodes are their untrusted-monitoring collusion risk; the refuted H6 is one operating point on their safety-usefulness curve, not a verdict on the architecture.
- *Difference.* Their monitor inspects code; ours reconstructs facts from records kept by three parties with different incentives, and the "attack policy" is a human-written false claim rather than an optimised model.
- *Reuse.* Plot review rate against liable-party accuracy and fooled rate across abstention thresholds (0.5 to 0.9) as a safety-usefulness curve; paraphrase or redact the agent's stated reasons before the fact-finder sees them, both to break collusion and to test whether accuracy depends on the agent's self-report; seed honeypot cases with known outcomes to calibrate fact-finders, as their upfront evaluation of the monitor does.

## 4. Positioning statement

The agent-market literature (Magentic Marketplace, Allouah et al.) measures how buying agents are steered before a purchase, and the injection benchmarks (AgentDojo, WASP, CaMeL) measure whether an agent carries out an attacker's goal; neither studies what happens after a purchase goes wrong, who pays, or what record would decide it. The LLM-judge and adjudication literature (Zheng et al., JudgeDeceiver, One-Token, CyberJurors, DRAssist, UMA) evaluates judges on preference data or on human disputes, with adversaries that are either optimisation artefacts or absent, and without a liability rule or a record the judge can verify cryptographically. The oversight literature (Irving et al., Khan et al., Kenton et al., Greenblatt et al.) studies weak judges of strong agents under incentives to deceive, but on reading comprehension, mathematics and code, where evidence is a quote and ground truth an answer key. ABS sits where these three meet: an arbiter must reconstruct what an AI agent did and whether it stayed inside a signed mandate, from a record whose parts are kept by three parties who each gain from lying, with ground truth from the simulator and a liability rule that decides who pays on an existing payment rail. Its measured contribution is therefore not a judge architecture (the hybrid lost on consistency and accuracy) but the finding that the content of the record, not the design of the judge, moves liable-party accuracy from 61-82% to 96-98%, together with the taxonomy, rules of evidence and decision order that turn such a record into an allocation the rails can execute.

## 5. Overlap risk: claimed contributions already covered elsewhere

Ordered by how much of the claim the prior work removes.
1. **Typed fields, `UntrustedText`, a monitor that sees only typed data, and "exact by construction".** CaMeL's design, and CaMeL proves a property we cannot: our arbiter's control flow is data-dependent by nature. Concede the design, claim only the application, and never use "provable" or "by design" for the Decision Center.
2. **Phase 1 as evidence about agent robustness.** Magentic Marketplace (frontier models resist seller manipulation; weaker models are redirected) and Anthropic's 1.4%-at-100-attempts result cover it with stronger attackers and more models. Our contribution is the older-model failure stopped by card-style mandate enforcement, and the metrics; the frontier null is confirmation, not a finding.
3. **"LLM extracts facts, deterministic rules decide" as a novel adjudication pattern.** DRAssist (structured elements, then decision), the pending neuro-symbolic legal-reasoning lane, and non-academic rules engines (Visa RDR, Compelling Evidence 3.0, noted in Audit 0) make this a design pattern with precedent. The measured result (strict hybrid 54% review, less consistent than A1) is new; the pattern is not.
4. **Cross-family quorum and correlated errors.** Project Vend 2 observes "same model, same blind spots"; Greenblatt et al. study monitor collusion; the ensemble-judging literature (pending lane) likely measures cross-model error correlation. Our 1.24x and 2.45x correlation figures are a contribution only when placed against that literature.
5. **Adversarial evidence against an LLM judge.** JudgeDeceiver (88-99% ASR) and One-Token exist; our hand-written adversaries are weaker. Do not claim the first adversarial evaluation of LLM judges; claim adversarial parties inside a liability procedure, and run an optimised attack before claiming robustness.
6. **Evidence-level ablation (E0 to E3).** Khan et al.'s quotes vs arguments-only and Kenton et al.'s QA with and without the article are the same experimental move (give the judge verifiable material or not). Our novelty is the mapping to real dispute records and rail evidence, not the ablation.
7. **Agents refusing to deceive for a principal (20 of 20).** The debate literature gets models to argue assigned wrong answers routinely by framing; our refusal rate is a prompting regime, not a property. State it as such (the paper already does).
8. **LLM adjudication of e-commerce disputes.** CyberJurors (ICML 2026, 6,000 real cases) exists. The gap statement must keep all four qualifiers: AI-agent purchases, adversarial parties, ground truth, liability rule.
9. **Tier 1 as debate.** Irving et al. is the protocol; our tier 1 (parties choose positions, rule-based acceptance) is an open protocol in Kenton et al.'s sense, and should be named as such rather than as a novel settlement stage.
10. **Reason codes, decision order, rail-neutral settlement.** Non-academic overlap (Visa 4.1.23, Amex, PayPal, AP2's out-of-scope statement) is covered in Audit 0 and conceded in `main.md`; no academic work found takes it.

## 6. Top 10 improvements to the experiment and the standard, ranked

1. **Run an optimised attack on the arbiter** (JudgeDeceiver-style suffixes in the merchant reply or listing, optimised against an open-weight fact-finder and transferred to Claude and Grok), plus One-Token "master key" claims, and report ASR against attempts as Anthropic does. Until then the fooled rate (1/20 at E3) is a lower bound and "never deceived" (A2, 0/75) is untested.
2. **Turn H6 into a safety-usefulness curve** (Greenblatt et al.): sweep the abstention confidence from 0.5 to 0.9 and plot review rate against liable-party accuracy and fooled rate for A1, A2, A3 and the single-finder hybrids. The pre-registered 0.7 point refuted H6; the curve answers the real question, at what review budget the hybrid pays.
3. **Run debate and consultancy as specified by Khan and Kenton:** assigned sides, a buyer advocate and a merchant advocate with private information, judge with the package only; a consultancy baseline; Elo over advocates; accuracy conditional on whether the protagonist is right. This is the Anthropic ask and the one place our setting has a structural edge (real information asymmetry with a known answer).
4. **Adopt the field's metric vocabulary:** WASP's intermediate vs end-to-end ASR for the red-team; AgentDojo's benign utility, utility under attack and targeted ASR over user-task x injection-task cases for Phase 1; Zheng's swap-and-agree consistency for position bias.
5. **Replace the refusing attacker.** 23 of 80 red-team rounds had no payload because the attacker refused; use a human red team or an unrestricted open model, at least 100 attempts per target and goal, before any Phase 1 robustness sentence.
6. **Add a third model family and measure error correlation against the ensemble-judging literature;** redact or paraphrase the agent's stated reasons before fact-finding (Greenblatt) to test whether arbiter accuracy depends on the agent's self-report, which is the faithfulness question Research Question 2 names.
7. **Position and order probes:** swap buyer claim and merchant reply, randomise evidence order, report consistency (JudgeDeceiver's positional attack consistency is a ready metric).
8. **Port Phase 1 into AgentDojo task format (MIT) or run it in Magentic Marketplace (MIT)** with their six manipulation strategies and a seller-agent rewrite loop (Allouah) scored on a continuous share metric, so the agent-side results are comparable with published numbers.
9. **Ground case realism and ground truth outside the author:** sample claim language and dispute-type mix from VerdictBench (6,000 real cases; licence to be read), complete the blind expert labelling (Amendment 11), and report expert-ground-truth agreement beside Zheng's human-human agreement as the ceiling.
10. **Write CaMeL's exclusions into the standard as the residual threat model:** text-to-text manipulation and data-dependent control flow are where the arbiter decides contested facts; scope "exact by construction" to the rule step; keep the count of LLM-only Findings (14 across 10 of 28 rules) as the published manipulation surface, and attach provenance labels to every Finding as CaMeL attaches capabilities to values.
